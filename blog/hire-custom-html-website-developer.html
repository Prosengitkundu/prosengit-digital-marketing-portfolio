Hiring a Custom HTML Website Developer: What to Put in the Brief

Learn how to brief a freelance HTML website developer, define deliverables, test a responsive site and protect ownership of your assets.

A business website project can become confusing before a line of code is written. One person expects a simple set of service pages; another assumes the quote includes copywriting, online payments, a content management system and years of maintenance. A clear brief helps a freelance developer explain what is feasible, estimate the work and identify decisions that belong to the business owner.

Hiring a custom HTML website developer generally means commissioning a site built with HTML, CSS and JavaScript rather than relying entirely on a prebuilt theme. That description alone does not define how content will be edited, how forms will work or who will maintain the site. This guide explains the questions to resolve before requesting a quote, how to compare proposals and what to test before accepting delivery.

The existing HTML versus WordPress comparison provides background on the technology choice. The right solution depends on your content, team and requirements; a hand-coded site is not automatically better for every business, and a content management system is not automatically slower or less secure.

Start with the website's job

Describe what a visitor should be able to understand and do. A small consultancy may need to explain three services, establish credibility and receive quote requests. A local business may need accurate service areas, hours and a contact path. A company running advertising may need a focused landing page and a tested inquiry form. These are different projects even if all are called a business website.

Identify the primary audience, the markets you can actually serve and the main action you want visitors to take. Then list secondary needs, such as accessibility, language versions, a portfolio or downloadable information. Avoid claiming that a feature is essential simply because another website has it. Each feature adds design, implementation, testing and maintenance work.

Document the current situation too. Is there an existing website with pages that should remain accessible? Does the business own its domain and hosting account? Are there important forms, analytics or integrations that must continue working? A developer needs this context to plan a safe transition rather than treating the project as an isolated set of visual mockups.

Define the pages and content responsibilities

A page inventory can include the homepage, service pages, about page, portfolio, contact page and any legally required information. For each page, state its purpose, the audience, the content owner and whether it is new, retained or updated. An existing site may also have articles whose URLs and content must be preserved.

Clarify who writes and approves copy. A developer can implement text supplied by the business, but that does not automatically include interviewing subject experts, verifying claims or writing long-form articles. If copywriting is included, specify the number of pages, the research process, revisions and the person who approves final service descriptions. The business remains responsible for checking prices, credentials, availability and other factual claims.

Gather assets early: the official logo, approved colors and typography, photographs the business has permission to use, contact details and relevant policy text. Do not ask the developer to invent testimonials, client logos or project results to fill an empty section. If authentic material is not ready, agree whether the launch should wait or whether a neutral, accurate presentation is appropriate.

Decide how content will be maintained

A static HTML site can be a good fit when content changes are relatively infrequent and the business has a dependable way to make updates. It can also become inconvenient if staff need to publish articles or change product information every day without technical assistance. Ask who will edit content after launch, how changes will be reviewed and what access they need.

A developer might offer a simple editing workflow, a separate content management system or ongoing support. Each option has costs and responsibilities. A CMS can make editing easier, but it also requires configuration, security updates and user management. Editing source files directly may be practical for a technically comfortable team but unsuitable for others.

Test the proposed workflow with a realistic task. Ask the developer to demonstrate how an authorized person would update a service description or add an article, and how the change reaches the live site. Confirm whether the business can recover an earlier version if a mistake is made. A maintenance decision should be based on actual staff capacity, not just the technology's label.

Specify functionality in observable terms

Instead of asking for a “modern interactive website,” describe the behavior a visitor and an administrator need. For a contact form, identify the required fields, destination, validation messages, confirmation state and what happens if delivery fails. If the form uses a third-party service, document its account owner, cost and privacy implications.

For appointment booking, payments, maps or customer accounts, identify the specific service or integration and who controls it. A website developer may be able to connect a third-party tool, but the business must confirm its subscription, policies and operational process. Do not assume that a form submission automatically creates a booked appointment or a completed sale.

Describe any browser and device requirements. Ask for responsive layouts at agreed screen sizes, keyboard-operable navigation, readable text and reasonable behavior when a script or image fails. Accessibility requirements should be explicit: a developer can implement specific practices and testing, but a formal conformance audit may require a separate specialist and scope.

Agree on visual preservation or design work

If you already have a brand or an existing website, tell the developer what must remain unchanged. Provide the official logo, color values, typefaces, existing page examples and any restrictions. If the task is an enhancement rather than a redesign, list the layout, navigation, content and interactions that must be preserved.

If new design is required, define the review stages. Will the developer provide wireframes, a visual mockup or a working prototype? How many revision rounds are included? Who can approve the design? A responsive implementation may need different arrangements at different widths, so review mobile and desktop layouts before the build is considered complete.

Avoid using a competitor's website as a template to copy. Examples can communicate preferences, but the final design and content should respect rights and accurately represent the business. The developer should not add fabricated testimonials, invented team members or unsupported claims simply to match an example layout.

Include search and content foundations

A website can be built with clean, crawlable HTML and still need a thoughtful content strategy. Agree on basic search-related deliverables: unique page titles and descriptions where appropriate, one clear main heading per page, descriptive links, sensible image alternatives, canonical URLs and a sitemap if the site requires one. These are implementation tasks, not guarantees of indexing or rankings.

For an existing site, preserve important URLs where possible. If a URL must change, map the old address to the appropriate new destination and test redirects before and after launch. Do not send every old page to the homepage without considering its content and user purpose. Record the migration plan, internal link updates and any pages intentionally removed.

Search engines need access to the content intended for discovery. If a page's main information only appears after a script runs, discuss the implications and test the delivered HTML. Check that staging restrictions are not accidentally applied to production. The existing SEO-friendly website guide gives additional context for these foundations.

Discuss performance without buying a score

Performance work should focus on the experience of real visitors, not only a single synthetic score. Ask how the developer will handle image dimensions and formats, third-party scripts, font loading and unnecessary code. Choose representative pages and devices for testing, and record the conditions used.

A large image may slow a page, but replacing it without considering visual quality or meaning can damage the design. Removing a third-party script may improve a test result but break analytics, booking or consent functionality. The developer should explain trade-offs and coordinate changes with the owner of each integration.

Agree on a reasonable testing and remediation process rather than demanding a universal score guarantee. Network conditions, devices, hosting and later content changes can affect results. The existing website speed guide explains why performance and usability should be considered together.

Establish security and privacy responsibilities

A static site may have a smaller server-side surface than a complex application, but it is not automatically risk-free. Forms, third-party scripts, domain access, hosting credentials and any backend integrations still need attention. Ask who manages updates, backups, account access and incident response after launch.

The business should own its domain and hosting accounts and use individual access where possible. Do not send passwords in a project brief. Grant the developer only the permissions needed and remove them when they are no longer required. If a third-party form or analytics service processes personal data, confirm who owns that account and seek appropriate privacy advice for the markets served.

Specify what is included in testing. A developer can check a contact form and basic configuration, but a formal penetration test or legal compliance assessment is a separate specialist service unless explicitly agreed. Do not represent a basic launch checklist as a guarantee of security or legal compliance.

Compare quotes by scope and dependencies

Give each developer the same page inventory, content responsibilities, features, visual requirements and timeline constraints. Ask for a breakdown of discovery, design, development, integration, testing, deployment and handover. A lower quote may exclude copywriting, paid tools, hosting or post-launch support; compare the actual deliverables rather than the headline price alone.

Clarify the payment schedule, revision limits, change-request process and what happens if the client supplies content late. Fixed-price work can be appropriate for a well-defined scope, while evolving requirements may need a different arrangement. Ask the developer to identify assumptions and exclusions so that a new feature does not appear unexpectedly during the final review.

The site's pricing page provides published starting points for relevant services, but the final quote should be based on the project's actual requirements. Avoid relying on a generic price range from another market as if it were a quote for your business.

Plan testing and acceptance before launch

A useful acceptance checklist describes observable outcomes. For example: the navigation works at agreed screen widths; each approved page loads; the contact form sends a test message to the correct destination; important links and images resolve; and the business can access its domain and hosting accounts. Include browser and device coverage in the agreement rather than assuming every possible combination is tested.

Test the content as well as the code. Verify service descriptions, prices, contact details, image alternatives and page titles. Ask the business owner to approve factual claims and policy text. If the project replaces an existing website, check a representative set of old URLs and redirects and preserve existing content that was not approved for removal.

Record defects, their severity, the person responsible and the retest result. Agree how long the client has to review the site and what happens if new requests fall outside the original scope. A launch should not depend on an informal statement that the site “looks fine” on one laptop.

Protect ownership and plan the handover

The business should receive the source files, agreed design assets, account and integration inventory, deployment instructions and any documentation needed for routine updates. Confirm rights to use fonts, images, plugins and other third-party assets. A developer can use licensed material only within the terms of that license; ownership of the website does not automatically transfer every third-party right.

Document how the site is built and deployed, where backups are kept, who can restore a previous version and how an authorized person can make a small content change. If ongoing maintenance is offered, define response expectations, included updates and the process for additional work. The business should be able to change providers without losing its domain or website files.

A handover meeting is useful when it demonstrates a real task. Ask to see how a service description is updated, reviewed and published, or how a form notification is checked. Record unresolved issues and the responsible owner. The handover is complete only when the business can access the agreed assets, not merely when a link to the live site is sent.

Make launch and rollback responsibilities explicit

A technically complete website is not necessarily ready to launch. The domain may still point to an old host, a form provider may need account verification, or the business may be waiting for final approval of a policy page. Prepare a launch checklist that identifies the sequence, the person authorized to make each change and the information needed to confirm success. Agree on a suitable launch window when both the developer and the business can check critical paths.

Before changing domain or hosting settings, confirm that the business controls the relevant accounts and that a restorable copy of the previous site exists. Record the current configuration and the intended changes. Domain and DNS updates can take time to propagate, so plan how the team will monitor the transition rather than promising an exact worldwide cutover time. If email uses the same domain, avoid changing records without understanding their role.

After deployment, test the public site rather than relying only on staging. Check key pages, navigation, images, forms, canonical links and a sample of redirects. Confirm that any test inquiries are identifiable and reach the intended recipient. If a serious problem occurs, the team should know who can authorize rollback and how to restore the previous version. Document remaining noncritical issues separately, with an owner and an agreed follow-up date.

This process is especially important when preserving an established site. A new visual design should not silently remove an old article, change a working contact address or disconnect an existing measurement tool. Inventory those dependencies before the launch and check them again afterward.

A hypothetical project brief

Imagine a small B2B consultancy with an existing five-page website. It wants to clarify two services, preserve its current articles and improve the contact path. The owner supplies approved copy and a logo; the developer is asked to implement a responsive design consistent with the existing branding, preserve article URLs and test the contact form.

The brief specifies that a third-party form account belongs to the business, that no new CMS is included and that the owner will approve factual claims. The developer provides a staging version, a redirect inventory for any changed URL and a checklist for desktop, tablet and mobile review. Any request for appointment booking is treated as a separately scoped integration.

After the owner approves the content, the team tests the site and deploys the agreed changes. The handover includes source files, access information and instructions for a routine update. This is a fictional example of a well-defined project, not a claim about a previous client or an outcome.

Questions to ask a prospective developer

Ask how the developer will turn the brief into a scope, what assumptions they need to verify and which work they do not perform. Request a clear explanation of the editing workflow, responsive testing, form integration, source ownership and deployment. Ask how they will preserve existing URLs and content if the project involves a redesign or migration.

Discuss communication and review. Who is the project contact, how are decisions recorded and how will changes outside the agreement be quoted? If the developer identifies a security, accessibility or legal issue outside their expertise, how will it be escalated? A clear boundary is more useful than an unsupported promise that every risk is covered.

Finally, ask what support is available after launch and what the business must do itself. A good answer identifies the deliverables and responsibilities in writing. The best project is one the business can operate and maintain, not simply one that looks attractive on launch day.

Frequently asked questions

Is a custom HTML website always better than WordPress?

No. The right choice depends on editing needs, functionality, maintenance and the team's capabilities. Compare the trade-offs for your project.

Can I update a static website without a developer?

Possibly, depending on the editing and deployment workflow. Ask for a demonstration and clear instructions before choosing the approach.

Does a website quote include copywriting and images?

Only if stated. Specify who supplies, licenses, writes and approves each asset and whether revisions are included.

Can a developer guarantee search rankings?

No. Technical foundations support discovery, but indexing and rankings depend on many factors and cannot be guaranteed.

Who should own the domain and hosting?

The business should retain control of its accounts and grant the developer appropriate access for the agreed work.

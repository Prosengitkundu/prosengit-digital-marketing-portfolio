International SEO: How to Get Customers in the USA, UK, Canada and Beyond

Ranking in another country takes more than translation. Targeting, site structure, hreflang, local signals and content strategy — the complete international SEO playbook.

Your website can be found from anywhere — but being found is not the same as being visible. A site that ranks well in one country is often invisible in another, even for the same language. International SEO is the discipline of making your website rank in the specific countries and languages where your customers live — whether you are a business in Bangladesh targeting clients in the USA, a UK company expanding to Europe, or a SaaS brand going global.

This guide covers the complete playbook: how to choose target markets, structure your site, handle languages and hreflang, build local relevance, and avoid the expensive mistakes I see most often.

What international SEO actually is (and isn't)

International SEO is standard SEO plus targeting: every foundation — technical health, keyword research, on-page optimization, content, authority — still applies. The additional layer is helping Google understand which country and language each page serves, and making each version genuinely relevant to its audience.

What it is not: automatic translation of your homepage, stuffing country names into footers, or creating dozens of near-identical "SEO USA / SEO UK / SEO Canada" doorway pages. Those tactics fail because they serve search engines instead of customers. Google's guidance is consistent: build the best result for the searcher in each market, and make the targeting technically clear.

Step 1: Choose target markets deliberately

Do not target "everywhere." Start with one to three markets where demand, competition and your ability to serve all align:

  • Demand: use keyword research set to each candidate country. A service with strong US search volume may have almost none in smaller markets — and vice versa.
  • Competition: the United States and United Kingdom are the most competitive English-language markets. Canada, Australia, Ireland and New Zealand often offer faster wins for the same effort.
  • Ability to serve: timezones, payment methods, contracts and support. Ranking in a market you cannot serve profitably is worse than useless.
  • Evidence you already have: check Search Console and Analytics by country. Existing impressions from a market are the cheapest possible demand signal.

Step 2: Pick the right site structure

For most small and mid-sized businesses, the choice is between three patterns:

Subfolders (example.com/us/, example.com/uk/) — recommended default

All authority consolidates on one domain, management stays simple, and new markets launch fast. This is the right choice for the vast majority of businesses starting international SEO.

Subdomains (us.example.com) — rarely ideal

Search engines often treat subdomains as semi-separate entities, splitting authority for little benefit. Useful only when technical constraints force separation.

Country domains (example.us, example.co.uk) — for committed expansion

The strongest geo-signal, but each domain builds authority from zero and multiplies maintenance. Justified when a market deserves a dedicated brand presence and budget — not as a starting move.

Step 3: Handle language and hreflang correctly

Hreflang tags tell Google which page version serves which language and region, preventing duplicate-content issues between similar versions (US vs UK English, for example). Practical rules:

  • Implement hreflang bidirectionally — every version references every other version, including itself.
  • Use language-region codes accurately (en-us, en-gb, fr-fr) — a wrong code is worse than none.
  • Keep it maintainable: hreflang via sitemap or HTTP headers scales better than page-level tags on large sites.
  • Never rely on hreflang alone — distinct URLs, localized content and local signals do the real work; hreflang resolves the ambiguity.

Important: US and UK English pages must differ substantively — spelling, terminology, examples, pricing, contact details — not just the hreflang tag. Thin "localization" is a doorway-page risk.

Step 4: Research keywords per market, not once globally

Search behavior differs by country more than most businesses expect:

  • Vocabulary: "lawyer" vs "solicitor," "plumber" phrasing, "cell phone" vs "mobile phone" — research each market's real terms.
  • Volume and difficulty: always measured per country. Track them separately; a global average misleads.
  • SERP layout: the same query can trigger map packs, shopping units or different feature mixes by country. Study each market's actual results page.
  • Local modifiers: service × city combinations ("emergency plumber Austin") are usually the highest-ROI targets for location businesses expanding abroad.

The research method itself is covered step by step in the keyword research guide — apply it once per target market.

Step 5: Build local relevance signals

Rankings follow relevance. For each target market, strengthen the signals Google associates with local trust:

  • Local contact details, service areas and delivery/shipping information stated clearly on market pages.
  • Local testimonials and case context (genuine ones only — never invent market-specific proof).
  • Local business listings and citations where a physical or service-area presence exists.
  • Content referencing the market naturally — regulations, seasons, industry norms, local examples.
  • Currency, units, date formats and spelling matched to the market.

Common international SEO mistakes

  1. Auto-translated clone pages with no real localization — thin, error-prone and risky.
  2. Blocking international visitors with forced redirects. Always let users (and crawlers) reach any version; suggest, don't force.
  3. One keyword list for all markets — ignoring vocabulary and competition differences.
  4. No local proof — expecting a generic page to outrank businesses embedded in the market.
  5. Expanding to five markets at once — win one market properly, systematize, then replicate.

How to start: a realistic 90-day plan

  1. Days 1–14: pick one target market; run per-country keyword research; audit technical readiness.
  2. Days 15–45: build or localize the market hub (service pages, contact proof, currency, spelling); implement hreflang.
  3. Days 46–90: publish market-specific supporting content; earn first local mentions; track impressions and average position per country in Search Console.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need a separate website for each country?

No — subfolders on your existing domain are the recommended starting point for most businesses. Separate country domains are for committed, well-funded expansion, not first steps.

How long does international SEO take?

Similar to standard SEO: technical and targeting fixes show effect in weeks; content-driven gains mature over 2–4 months per market. New markets start from lower authority, so patience plus consistent publishing wins.

Can a freelancer manage international SEO remotely?

Yes — research, technical implementation guidance, content planning and reporting are all location-independent. What matters is per-market research discipline and honest reporting, which is exactly how my SEO services are structured.

Should I translate my site or rewrite it per market?

Rewrite key commercial pages per market; translation (human-reviewed, never raw machine output) can support the rest. US/UK English versions must differ substantively to avoid doorway-page risk.

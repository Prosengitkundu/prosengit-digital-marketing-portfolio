Brand Identity Design Packages: What Should Be Included?

Compare brand identity design packages by strategy, logo variations, colors, typography, applications, rights and practical handover.

A business can have a logo and still present itself inconsistently. One document uses a different typeface, a social profile uses an outdated color, and the website header contains a stretched version of the mark. A visual identity package can help establish a small, usable system so that different materials feel connected without requiring a designer to approve every routine update.

Brand identity design packages vary considerably. One may include a logo, a color palette and a short usage sheet. Another may include discovery, several logo arrangements, typography, stationery, social templates and a more detailed guide. The package name alone does not tell you what you will receive or whether it fits your business.

This guide helps owners compare proposals, prepare a useful brief and plan a handover. It focuses on visual identity rather than claiming that design alone will create a complete business strategy or guarantee customer trust. A credible identity must be supported by an accurate offer and a consistent customer experience.

Understand the difference between brand and visual identity

A brand includes the associations people form from their experience with a business: its service, communication, reliability and many other factors. A visual identity is the set of design elements used to represent that business. Those elements can support recognition and clarity, but they cannot replace a service the business does not deliver.

A logo is one element of a visual identity. Colors, typography, image choices, layout and tone of presentation can help the logo work consistently across a website, proposal, social profile or printed document. The appropriate level of detail depends on the business's size, channels and ability to maintain the system.

When comparing packages, ask the provider to define its terms. “Brand strategy” might mean a short discovery conversation, a written positioning exercise or a more extensive research project. “Brand guidelines” might mean a single page of logo rules or a detailed document covering multiple applications. Neither is inherently wrong, but the client should know what is included.

Begin with the business and audience

A useful brief describes the actual service, intended customers, markets and the ways people encounter the business. A local service provider may need signs and printed materials; an international consultant may rely on a website, video calls and proposals. The identity should support those real uses rather than exist only as an attractive presentation.

Explain what the business wants to communicate and what must remain true. If it is an individual freelancer, do not design materials that imply a large agency or a local office in another country. If the business has an established audience, identify existing elements that customers recognize and decide whether they should be retained.

Share relevant constraints such as an existing logo, a required color, a long business name or a website design that must not be changed. Name the person who can approve the work and gather stakeholder input before the designer develops concepts. A clear brief reduces the risk of choosing a visual direction that cannot be used in the business's real channels.

Scope discovery and research honestly

Discovery can include reviewing the current website and materials, discussing the audience, identifying practical applications and agreeing on design goals. It may also include a limited review of comparable businesses to understand the visual context. Ask the designer to state the methods, deliverables and limitations.

A visual review of competitors does not establish their sales, customer satisfaction or legal rights. It should not lead to copying another business's mark or claiming that a particular color guarantees a commercial result. The purpose is to understand the environment and make informed design choices for your own business.

If customer interviews, market research or a formal positioning exercise are required, specify who will conduct them, how participants will be selected and what the business will receive. Those activities may be separate from visual design. Avoid paying for a package label that sounds comprehensive while the actual deliverables remain undefined.

Define the logo system and its variations

A visual identity may include a primary logo and alternate arrangements for different spaces. A horizontal version can fit a website header, while a stacked or simplified mark may be more suitable for a square profile image. Variations should be designed as a coherent system, not produced by arbitrarily stretching or cropping the primary mark.

Ask which versions are included and where each should be used. Check legibility at the actual sizes relevant to the business. A detailed design may need a simplified small-scale version; a long name may require careful spacing. If the business needs a one-color version for printing or engraving, include that requirement in the brief.

A logo design is not automatically legally protected. Originality, ownership and trademark registration are related but distinct issues. Ask the designer to disclose third-party elements and consult a qualified intellectual-property professional if the business needs a legal search or registration in relevant jurisdictions.

Choose colors for real applications

A palette should include the agreed primary and supporting colors and a practical explanation of their use. Consider how colors appear on the website, in documents, on social graphics and in print. A screen preview is not a guarantee that a physical printer or material will reproduce the same appearance.

Ask for appropriate color specifications for the intended uses and check contrast for text and interface elements. A palette can be visually appealing while making small text hard to read. The website developer and designer should coordinate on actual components, not assume that a decorative palette automatically meets accessibility needs.

Limit the system to what the team can use consistently. A large palette with no guidance can encourage arbitrary choices. A concise set of roles—such as primary, accent, background and text—may be more useful for a small business, provided the colors support the necessary applications and are tested in context.

Agree on typography and licensing

Typography affects headings, body text, documents and graphics. The designer should identify the proposed typefaces, their roles and any licensing requirements. A font available for one use is not necessarily licensed for every website, document, product or distribution method. The client should know whether a separate license is needed and who is responsible for it.

Test typography with real content. Long service names, small labels and paragraphs on mobile devices can expose readability problems that are not visible in a short mockup. If the existing website's typography must be preserved, state that requirement; a new identity project should not silently change the site's established design.

Provide fallbacks where appropriate. A business may need to share editable documents with people who do not have a particular font installed. The usage guide can explain which typeface is used in professionally produced assets and what staff should use in everyday documents.

Define imagery and graphic elements

An identity package may include guidance for photography, illustrations, icons, patterns or other supporting graphics. Ask whether the designer is creating original assets, selecting licensed material or providing only recommendations. Confirm the rights and any restrictions on editing or distribution.

The visual style should fit the business without inventing evidence. Stock or generated imagery should not be represented as a real client project, office or testimonial. If the business uses photographs of people or customer premises, obtain appropriate permission and protect privacy. A consistent style is valuable only when the underlying representation is honest.

Consider how the team will produce future materials. A complex illustration system may be difficult to maintain without ongoing design support. A small set of flexible templates and clear examples may be more practical. The package should match the organization's resources rather than adding assets that nobody can use.

Specify applications and templates

List the applications needed at launch: website logo, social profile image, presentation cover, proposal document, business card or other materials. For each, identify the content, dimensions or supplier requirements and who will maintain it. A mockup showing a logo on many objects is not the same as production-ready files for those objects.

If social media templates are included, specify the number, formats, editable software and intended uses. A template for an announcement may need different text space from one for an educational post. Test it with realistic content and ensure that staff can edit it without breaking the layout or using unlicensed assets.

For print materials, coordinate with the printer before final approval. Bleed, trim, color and file requirements vary by supplier. The designer should prepare the agreed files, but the client and production vendor should verify proofs and quantities. Printing and shipping costs are not automatically included in a design fee.

Make guidelines practical for the people using them

A brand guide should answer everyday questions: which logo version should I use, what colors and typefaces are approved, how much space should surround the mark, and where can I find the files? It can also show examples of acceptable backgrounds and common mistakes. The depth should match the package and the complexity of the business.

Use clear file names and a business-controlled folder. Separate final assets from unused concepts and working drafts so staff do not accidentally use an outdated version. If the guide references a template or font, include the location and any license information. A long manual is not useful if the team cannot find the correct file.

Demonstrate one or two routine tasks during handover. For example, show how to select the correct logo for a presentation or update an editable social graphic. Ask the business to try the task and identify questions. A practical walkthrough can reveal gaps that a polished PDF alone does not address.

Understand ownership, rights and approvals

Agree in writing on ownership and usage rights for the final design, source files and any licensed elements. Clarify when rights transfer, whether unused concepts remain with the designer and whether the business can modify the assets. A paid project does not automatically transfer every right associated with a third-party font, photograph or stock illustration.

The business should approve the exact spelling of its name, service descriptions and any claims used in applications. The designer should disclose dependencies and provide the agreed documentation. If the project requires trademark clearance, engage a qualified professional rather than relying on a visual similarity check alone.

Keep approval records and final files accessible to the business. If an employee or provider leaves, the organization should still be able to use its identity. Store credentials separately from design files and use individual access for shared tools where possible.

Compare packages using the same requirements

Provide each prospective designer with the same brief, applications, existing assets and deadline. Ask for a breakdown of discovery, concept directions, revisions, logo variations, colors, typography, templates, guidelines and handover. Identify any costs for fonts, stock assets, printing or website implementation.

Compare the deliverables and the process, not just the number of mockups. A concept presented on several objects may still be one design direction. A package with many files may not include the editable format your team needs. Ask how feedback is consolidated, how scope changes are handled and who approves the final version.

Be cautious of guarantees that a visual identity will produce a particular revenue result or secure legal protection. A professional can create a coherent system and explain its use, but commercial outcomes depend on the service and customer experience as well. Review the site's pricing information and request a quote for the actual scope.

Decide how feedback and revisions will work

A visual identity may affect many departments, but unlimited individual feedback can make the project difficult to finish. Name a decision-maker and agree which stakeholders need to review the work at each stage. Ask them to assess the concepts against the brief: audience, applications, legibility and the elements that must be preserved. Collect comments in one place and resolve conflicting preferences before sending them to the designer.

Define what counts as a revision. Refining the spacing in an approved logo arrangement may be different from requesting a new direction after the business changes its name. A written agreement should explain the included review rounds and how additional work is estimated. It should also allow the designer to correct a genuine defect in the agreed deliverable without treating that correction as an unrelated new request.

Review applications at realistic sizes rather than judging the identity only from a presentation. Place the proposed mark in the existing website header, use sample text in a document and print a proof if physical production matters. If a design does not work in one context, ask whether an approved alternate version or a change to the application can solve the problem. Document the decision so the same issue does not return during handover.

Finally, check the wording used in mockups. A visually convincing example should not contain invented credentials, customer names or results that could be mistaken for real claims. Replace sample content with approved business information before any material is published.

Plan the rollout across existing channels

Inventory the places where the current identity appears: website, social profiles, documents, email signatures, signage and printed materials. Decide which updates are included in the design engagement and which belong to a developer, printer or internal staff member. A new identity package does not automatically update every account.

If the website design must be preserved, coordinate with the developer on how the approved assets will fit the existing header, footer and responsive layouts. Test the logo at actual sizes and maintain navigation and functionality unless a change is approved. A favicon should be produced and tested as a separate small-scale asset when needed.

Plan the transition to avoid inconsistent files. Assign an owner for the approved folder and set a date for replacing templates where practical. If existing printed stock will remain in use temporarily, document the decision. A phased rollout can be sensible when immediate replacement would create unnecessary cost or waste.

A hypothetical identity package

Imagine a small consulting business with a website and several inconsistent proposal templates. The owner wants a more coherent visual identity but does not want a website redesign. The brief specifies the existing layout to preserve, the audience, the approved business name and the need for a logo that works in a narrow header.

The designer conducts a focused discovery conversation, presents agreed concept directions and refines the selected system. The package includes logo variations, a limited palette, typography guidance and an editable proposal cover. The owner confirms service wording and reviews the assets on a phone and in a printed proof.

A separate website update is scoped to replace the logo without changing navigation or content. The business receives the agreed files and a short usage guide. This fictional scenario illustrates a possible division of work; it is not a real client case study or a guarantee of results.

Check the handover before the project closes

Review the final asset list against the agreement. Confirm that the business name is correct, the files open in the expected tools and the approved variations are included. Test a representative application, such as placing the logo in a document or exporting a social template. Record any defect or missing item and agree on the correction process.

Confirm where source files and licensed assets are stored and who can access them. If the business needs ongoing design support, define that separately from the completed package. A clear handover lets the business maintain consistency or work with another provider without losing the ability to use its identity.

The best package is not necessarily the one with the most deliverables. It is the one that provides a coherent, accurate system for the channels the business actually uses and gives the people responsible enough guidance to maintain it.

Frequently asked questions

Is a logo the same as a brand identity?

No. A logo is one element; a visual identity may also include colors, typography, imagery, templates and usage guidance.

Are website changes included in a brand package?

Only if the proposal says so. Design assets and website implementation are often separate work with different responsibilities.

Can a designer guarantee trademark registration?

No. Consult a qualified intellectual-property professional for legal searches and registration advice.

Who owns the final files?

Ownership and usage rights should be agreed in writing, including any restrictions on third-party fonts, stock assets and unused concepts.

Do I need a long brand manual?

Not necessarily. The guide should be detailed enough for your team's real applications and practical enough to use.

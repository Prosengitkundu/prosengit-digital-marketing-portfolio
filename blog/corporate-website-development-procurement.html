Corporate Website Development Requirements: A Practical Procurement Brief

Prepare a corporate website development brief covering stakeholders, content, accessibility, integrations, migration and acceptance criteria.

A corporate website project often involves more than a new homepage. Marketing wants clearer service pages, operations needs accurate locations, leadership wants a credible presentation, and the people handling inquiries need forms that deliver to the right place. Without a shared brief, a developer may receive conflicting instructions and the business may discover important requirements only when launch is near.

A procurement brief turns those needs into a project that can be estimated, reviewed and accepted. It should describe the business purpose, audiences, page inventory, integrations, content responsibilities, technical requirements and decision-making process. It should also identify what is not included. A freelancer can help refine the plan, but the organization must supply the facts and approvals that only it controls.

This guide is for teams comparing corporate website development proposals. It does not prescribe a particular platform or claim that a new site will automatically produce leads. It focuses on the practical decisions that protect existing content and help the finished website serve the organization and its visitors.

Identify the business decisions behind the project

A request for a “modern corporate website” is a starting point, not a complete requirement. Explain why the current site is being changed. Are important services difficult to find? Is the contact path unclear? Can staff no longer maintain the content? Has the organization changed its structure or expanded into a new market? A developer needs to understand which problems the project is meant to address.

List the intended audiences and their tasks. A prospective client may need to compare services and request a discussion. A job applicant may need current opportunities and a way to apply. An existing customer may need support information. A partner may need a clear description of the business and its contact details. These journeys can overlap, but they should not be assumed to have the same priority.

Choose a small number of primary outcomes and describe how the team will assess them. A successful launch might mean that visitors can locate the correct service page and submit a working inquiry, while authorized staff can update contact information without developer intervention. Search traffic and sales are influenced by many factors, so avoid making a specific ranking or revenue figure the sole acceptance criterion.

Establish who decides and who contributes

Corporate projects can stall when several departments can request changes but nobody can approve a final version. Name a project owner, a technical contact, content owners and the person who can approve scope and spending. Identify who reviews legal, privacy, accessibility and brand requirements where those reviews are needed.

A responsibility table can make dependencies visible. Marketing may supply and approve service copy; operations may verify addresses and hours; a developer may implement the pages; an IT administrator may control domain settings. The project owner should resolve conflicting feedback and record decisions. A freelancer should not be expected to invent business facts or choose between contradictory stakeholder instructions without a designated decision-maker.

Agree how feedback will be collected. Consolidated comments with page references and clear priorities are easier to action than several separate email threads. Set review windows that reflect stakeholder availability, and describe what happens when a dependency is delayed. A realistic approval process is part of the schedule, not an afterthought.

Inventory the current website before proposing changes

List existing pages, articles, downloads, forms and integrations. Identify which URLs must remain accessible and which content is owned by each department. A site may contain old but valuable resources that are not obvious from the main navigation. Removing them without review can affect customers, internal links and search discovery.

For each page, record its purpose and a proposed action: retain, update, move, consolidate or remove after approval. If a URL will change, identify the intended destination and plan a redirect. A redirect should lead to a relevant equivalent where possible, not automatically to the homepage. Test the mapping and update internal links as part of the migration.

Gather baseline information without treating it as a guarantee of future results. Search and analytics reports can help identify important pages, but data may be incomplete or affected by tracking changes. Ask the developer or SEO specialist to document what was reviewed, what access was available and what needs further investigation. The existing SEO-friendly website guide covers related foundations.

Define the page and content model

A page inventory should describe the intended page types and the information each must contain. A service page may need an overview, scope, audience, process and contact action. A location page may need a verified address or service area, hours and local contact information. A corporate overview may explain the organization without adding unsupported claims about its size or achievements.

Specify who writes, edits, translates and approves content. A development quote may cover implementation of supplied copy but not interviews, legal review or long-form writing. If the supplier is asked to draft content, define the research process, revision rounds and factual approval. Do not use invented client logos, testimonials, certifications or project results to fill a design template.

Consider ongoing maintenance. Who will update a leadership biography, replace a document or publish a new service? If the team needs a content management system, specify the editing tasks and roles rather than simply naming a platform. If a static site is preferred, explain how authorized changes will be made and deployed. The right choice depends on the organization's capacity and requirements.

Specify functionality as user journeys

Describe features in terms of what users and staff need to do. For a contact form, define required fields, validation, notification recipients, confirmation behavior and a process for failed delivery. For a file download, identify the document owner and how an outdated version will be replaced. For a job application, specify whether a third-party recruitment system is involved and who controls it.

Document integrations such as analytics, booking, customer relationship management, email services and maps. Identify the account owner, the data exchanged, relevant costs and the person responsible for the connection. A developer can implement a defined integration, but the business must confirm its operational and privacy requirements.

Avoid vague requirements such as “integrate everything” or “make it future-proof.” If a feature is a possible future phase, mark it as such and ask whether the proposed architecture leaves a reasonable path for it. The initial project should include acceptance criteria for what is actually being delivered, not assumptions about unapproved future work.

Preserve branding and visual hierarchy

Provide current brand assets, typography, colors, approved imagery and examples of existing layouts that must be preserved. If the project is an enhancement, list the elements that should not change: navigation, header, footer, animations, service cards or other established components. If a redesign is authorized, define which parts of the identity remain fixed and which are open to exploration.

Review designs at the widths your audience uses. A desktop mockup alone cannot show how navigation, tables, forms or long headings behave on a small screen. Ask for a review process that includes representative desktop, tablet and mobile layouts. Maintain readable text, usable controls and clear visual hierarchy rather than simply shrinking the desktop page.

Agree on approval stages and revision limits. A wireframe may settle page structure before detailed visual work begins. A working prototype can reveal interaction issues that a static image cannot. Record decisions so that a late preference change can be evaluated as a change request rather than silently expanding the original scope.

Define accessibility and responsive requirements

A corporate site should be usable by people with different devices and abilities. Include requirements for keyboard navigation, visible focus, meaningful headings, form labels, text alternatives for informative images and sufficient contrast. Ask the developer to explain how these will be implemented and tested.

Use an established standard as a reference where appropriate. The Web Content Accessibility Guidelines provide a framework, but a claim of conformance requires an appropriately scoped evaluation. Specify whether the project includes basic checks, automated testing, manual testing or an independent accessibility audit. No single automated scan establishes that an entire site is accessible.

Responsive behavior should be tested with real content, including long service names, error messages and translated text if applicable. Check that menus and forms remain usable, images maintain their intended proportions and content does not overflow the viewport. Agree on representative browsers and devices; no proposal should imply that every possible configuration has been tested unless it actually has.

Address security, privacy and account ownership

The organization should retain control of its domain, hosting, analytics, content management system and third-party accounts. Use individual access and the minimum permissions needed for the project. Record who can approve access and who will remove it after the engagement. Avoid sharing passwords through a general project document.

Document the data the site collects and where it goes. A simple contact form may send personal information to an email service or CRM. The organization should decide what information is necessary and obtain appropriate privacy and legal advice for the markets it serves. The developer can implement an agreed configuration but should not be assumed to provide legal compliance advice.

Specify backups, update responsibilities and incident escalation. A static website can still depend on forms and third-party scripts; a CMS requires additional maintenance. Ask what security checks are included and what requires a separate specialist. A launch checklist is not a guarantee against every vulnerability.

Include search and performance foundations

Define basic SEO deliverables such as unique titles and descriptions where appropriate, crawlable page content, descriptive links, canonical URLs, a valid sitemap and a plan for redirects. Check that staging restrictions do not remain on production pages intended for discovery. These foundations support search engines and users but do not guarantee indexing, rankings or inquiries.

For performance, identify representative pages and testing conditions. Large images, font loading and third-party scripts can affect the experience, but removing a script may break a business process. Ask the developer to explain trade-offs and document the measurement method. A single lab score should not be treated as a complete description of every visitor's experience.

The website speed and SEO guide provides background. The procurement brief should focus on the actual pages and dependencies the team can test and maintain. If a specialized performance investigation is needed, define it separately from the basic launch scope.

Compare supplier proposals on equivalent terms

Send the same brief to prospective developers and ask them to identify assumptions, exclusions, dependencies and risks. Compare page types, design work, content migration, integrations, testing, deployment and handover. A quote for implementing supplied content is not equivalent to a quote that includes research, writing and stakeholder interviews.

Ask for a schedule that includes client review and approval, not just development time. Clarify payment milestones, revision limits and how changes to scope will be estimated. If the organization needs procurement or security review before access is granted, include that dependency in the timetable.

Do not select solely on a promised launch date or a generic price. Ask how the supplier will preserve existing content, test a form, handle a failed launch and transfer the source files. The site's pricing information can help frame a discussion, but the final quote should reflect the actual corporate project.

Create acceptance criteria before work begins

Acceptance criteria should be observable and linked to the scope. Examples include: approved pages load at their agreed URLs; existing articles remain accessible; navigation works with a keyboard; a test inquiry reaches the designated recipient; and authorized staff can perform the agreed content update. Specify the browsers, devices and test data used.

Identify who will test each item and how defects will be recorded. A content owner should verify factual information, while a technical owner checks integrations and deployment. Agree on severity definitions and whether a defect blocks launch. New feature requests should be separated from corrections to agreed work so the team can make an informed decision about scope.

After a fix, retest the affected journey and record the result. Keep evidence of approval and unresolved issues. A checklist that merely says “site approved” is less useful than a record of what was checked, by whom and under which conditions.

Plan migration, launch and rollback

Before launch, create a mapping of old and new URLs, confirm backups, document account access and schedule a launch window when the relevant people are available. Test the staging site with realistic content and verify that production configuration differs where necessary, including form destinations and search indexing settings.

The launch plan should name the person authorized to change domain or hosting settings and the person who will validate the public site. Check important pages, redirects, forms, images, metadata and connected tools after deployment. Domain changes may take time to propagate; do not promise an exact universal cutover time.

Prepare a rollback plan for serious failures. Identify the previous version, the steps to restore it and who can approve that decision. Keep a list of noncritical issues that can be addressed after launch, with owners and dates. A successful deployment is not the same as search indexing, and the report should distinguish the two.

A hypothetical corporate project

Imagine a professional services company with several service lines, existing articles and a contact form connected to a shared inbox. Marketing wants clearer navigation; operations needs accurate service locations; and the IT administrator controls the domain. The business hires a freelance developer to improve the site without changing the established brand.

The brief names a project owner, lists the pages to retain, identifies the form recipient and states that existing article URLs must remain accessible. Marketing supplies approved copy, operations verifies location details and the developer implements and tests the agreed changes. A separate decision is required before adding a new booking integration.

The team reviews desktop and mobile layouts, submits a controlled test inquiry and checks a sample of old URLs before launch. After deployment, the business receives source files and a short maintenance guide. This is a fictional example of coordination, not a claim about a real client or a promised outcome.

Resolve content and integration dependencies early

A project schedule should show when the developer needs approved material, not just when pages are expected to be finished. Ask each department to identify the source of its content and the person who can confirm that it remains accurate. If a service description depends on an upcoming business decision, mark it as unresolved and agree on a temporary approach rather than publishing an assumption.

Test external dependencies before the final launch week. A CRM administrator may need to create an account, a form service may require verification, and a domain owner may need time to approve a configuration change. If one of those tasks is delayed, decide whether the affected feature can be safely deferred or whether the launch should wait. Record the decision and the owner of the follow-up work.

A short dependency review at each project checkpoint can reveal problems while there is still time to act. This is particularly valuable when an independent freelancer must coordinate with several departments but does not control their schedules or accounts.

Make the handover operational

A corporate handover should include source files, deployment instructions, account and integration ownership, a page inventory, redirect mapping, test results and known issues. Where a CMS is used, document user roles and demonstrate the agreed editing tasks. Confirm rights to fonts, images and third-party components rather than assuming all assets transfer automatically.

Agree who maintains the site after launch. Routine updates, security patches, backups, content review and support requests may require different owners. If a freelancer provides maintenance, specify the work included and how additional requests are quoted. The organization should be able to change providers while retaining its domain, hosting and content.

A concise, accurate brief reduces avoidable uncertainty. It does not eliminate every change during a project, but it gives the team a shared basis for evaluating changes and protecting existing content. The goal is a website the organization can operate and maintain, not just a visually complete launch.

Frequently asked questions

What should a corporate website brief include?

Include business goals, audiences, page inventory, content owners, functionality, branding, technical requirements, acceptance criteria and launch responsibilities.

Does a corporate site need a CMS?

Not always. Choose an editing workflow based on how often content changes, staff capabilities, security responsibilities and the required features.

Who should approve website content?

Name an owner for each type of content and a final decision-maker. Legal, privacy or regulated claims may need review by qualified specialists.

Will a new website guarantee more leads?

No. A well-designed site can support a clearer customer journey, but inquiries depend on the offer, audience, traffic, follow-up and other factors.

What happens to existing articles during a redesign?

Inventory them before work starts, agree which must remain, preserve URLs where possible and test any approved redirects and internal links.

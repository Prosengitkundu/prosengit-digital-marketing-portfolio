Google Ads Consultation for Small Businesses: What to Bring

Prepare for a Google Ads consultation with clear goals, account ownership, conversion definitions and questions about next steps.

A small business may be considering Google Ads because it wants more inquiries, or it may already be spending money without understanding which activity is useful. A consultation can help frame the decision, but a short meeting cannot reveal every account issue or guarantee a profitable campaign. Its value comes from a clear question, reliable context and an honest discussion of what needs further work.

Google Ads consultation for a small business can cover an offer, target market, budget constraints, account ownership, conversion definitions and possible next steps. Depending on the agreement, a consultant may review a limited account sample before the meeting or provide a short action list afterward. Campaign setup, tracking implementation and ongoing management are separate responsibilities unless explicitly included.

This guide helps owners prepare for a focused consultation and compare providers. The existing Google Ads management guide provides broader campaign background; here the focus is the advisory engagement and the decisions a business can make from it.

Start with the business question

Define what you need to decide. Are you considering a first campaign? Do you want to understand whether an existing account's reported leads match the inquiries received? Are you changing providers and need a safe transition plan? Each question requires different information and may call for a different level of review.

Describe the service or product, the customers you can help, the locations you serve and the action you want a visitor to take. A local service business may need phone calls during staffed hours; a B2B consultant may prefer a detailed inquiry form. The consultant should understand how the business follows up before recommending a campaign objective.

List your constraints. A limited budget, a planned website launch or a team that cannot respond quickly can affect the recommendation. A campaign cannot compensate for an unavailable service or a form nobody monitors. The consultation should connect advertising to the actual customer journey.

Separate a consultation from an account audit

A short advisory session may review the business's goal and explain options. A detailed account audit can require access to campaigns, conversion settings, billing, landing pages and historical context. Ongoing management includes recurring decisions and approved changes. These are different services even when the same specialist offers them.

Ask whether the consultant will inspect the account before the call, which campaigns or date ranges are included and what the business will receive afterward. A sample review should be described as a sample. If a technical tracking investigation or a complete account restructuring is needed, request a separate scope and estimate.

A consultant can identify questions and risks without immediately changing the account. Avoid accepting a promise that a brief conversation will diagnose every issue or produce a fixed return. Clear boundaries help the business decide whether further work is justified.

Confirm account and billing ownership

The business should retain appropriate control of its advertising account, billing and related assets. Do not share a personal password for a consultation. If account access is needed, use the platform's permission system and grant the minimum role required for the agreed review. Google's account access guidance describes the current options.

Identify the administrator, the person who pays platform charges and any previous provider with access. If a manager account is linked, understand which assets it can manage and ensure the business can still access its own account. A consultation should not require surrendering ownership to the adviser.

Record permissions granted and remove unnecessary access when the engagement ends. If access is disputed or the business cannot find the owner, resolve that issue through the appropriate platform and contractual processes before making campaign changes. A new account is not automatically the right answer to a difficult handover.

Bring the offer and the landing page

Provide the URL that an advertisement would use and explain the actual offer. Does the page describe the service, the intended customer, location or delivery restrictions, and the next step? Does a form work on a phone? Can the business respond to a call or inquiry? A consultant needs this context to discuss whether advertising is a suitable next action.

Check that any proposed ad claim matches the page and the business's capabilities. Do not promise a price, deadline or outcome that has not been approved. If the page contains an outdated service or a broken contact path, the business may need to fix that issue before increasing spend.

The existing landing-page guide provides context for the visitor experience. A consultation may identify a page issue, but writing or developing a replacement page is separate work unless included in the agreement.

Define an inquiry and a qualified lead

A platform may report a form submission, a telephone-link click or another conversion action. These events are not interchangeable with a qualified prospect or a sale. Ask the business team what happens after each action and which information is needed to determine whether the inquiry is relevant.

For an existing account, identify the conversion actions currently used in reports or bidding. A consultant should ask whether a form event fires after a successful submission or merely when a button is clicked. A telephone click does not prove that a call connected. If the definitions are unclear, a tracking investigation may be a higher priority than changing bids or creative.

Protect personal information when discussing examples. Aggregate or anonymized data can often illustrate the problem without sharing customer names or contact details. Privacy and legal requirements vary; seek advice from an appropriately qualified professional when deciding how data should be collected and used.

Prepare budget information honestly

The consultant needs to know the budget range the business is willing to consider and whether it is separate from professional fees, website work and third-party tools. The advertising platform charges for delivery; a management or consultation fee pays for the agreed service. A proposal should distinguish them.

Do not assume that a fixed budget guarantees a fixed number of inquiries. Competition, targeting, the offer, landing page and other factors affect outcomes. A consultant can explain options and the limitations of a proposed test, but should not invent a universal cost per lead or return.

Ask how spending limits and approvals would work if the business proceeds. Who can change a campaign budget? What happens if the offer becomes unavailable? Who receives an alert about unexpected spend? These questions matter even when the immediate engagement is advisory rather than hands-on management.

Review the available account history

If an existing account is in scope, bring the relevant date range and information about recent changes. A budget adjustment, a redesigned website, a changed service area or a new conversion configuration can affect the reports. The consultant should ask about those events before attributing a change to one campaign setting.

A limited review might examine a sample of campaigns, conversion definitions, search terms and landing pages. The consultant should state what was not examined. If the account has many campaigns or linked systems, a short call may identify priorities but not provide a complete audit.

Ask for observations and evidence rather than a verdict based on one metric. A reported cost per conversion can be misleading if the conversion action includes unsuccessful forms or irrelevant inquiries. The business's follow-up records and the website's actual behavior may be necessary to interpret the account.

Discuss targeting and search intent

A search campaign should reflect the services and locations the business can genuinely support. Ask the consultant how they would investigate relevant searches, geographic settings and exclusions. A broad audience may produce unrelated inquiries, while an overly restrictive configuration may omit suitable customers. The appropriate choice depends on the offer and available evidence.

Search term reports and keyword settings can provide clues, but they do not reveal every aspect of a customer's intent. A negative keyword added without context can block useful searches. A consultant should explain the reason for a proposed change and whether the business's approval is required.

Do not expect a keyword list to solve a weak offer or a broken landing page. The consultation should consider the message, destination and follow-up as a connected process. If the specialist has not reviewed those components, the advice should be labeled preliminary.

Ask about measurement and privacy

A reliable plan defines what is measured, how the event is triggered and what limitations apply. If an inquiry form uses a third-party service, the developer or integration owner may need to participate in testing. A successful website submission should be verified separately from the platform report.

Discuss consent and data handling with the appropriate business and legal advisers. The consultant can implement or recommend an agreed technical configuration, but should not present a marketing consultation as legal compliance advice. Avoid sending unnecessary personal information to analytics or advertising systems.

If tracking is incomplete, agree on a safe next step. That may be a controlled test, a review of existing tags or a separate implementation project. Do not change a primary conversion action or automated bidding input without understanding the account's dependencies and obtaining approval.

Set expectations for the session and handover

Ask what preparation is included, how long the meeting lasts, who should attend and whether the business receives written notes. A useful summary can distinguish completed observations, open questions and recommended actions. Each action should name an owner or the role needed to carry it out.

A consultation may recommend a pause, a focused test, a landing-page improvement or a deeper account audit. The right answer depends on the evidence. Ask which recommendation is conditional on information that has not yet been verified and what the next investigation would cost.

Clarify follow-up. A question about the advice may be included for a limited period; campaign changes, additional account reviews and ongoing monitoring may be separately quoted. The business should be able to use the advice without feeling obliged to buy a retainer.

Compare consultants using the same brief

Provide prospective advisers with the same business goal, market, account status, budget constraints and landing page. Ask whether the proposal includes pre-call review, access requirements, a written handover and any implementation. Compare a short introductory discussion with another introduction, not with a detailed audit that requires substantial analysis.

Ask how the consultant handles uncertainty and which platform policies or official resources they will consult. A credible provider should explain the limits of their review and distinguish a recommendation from a tested change. They should also respect the business's account ownership and approval process.

Be cautious of guaranteed leads, a fixed return or claims of a special relationship with the platform. Advertising outcomes depend on the offer, market, competition, measurement and follow-up. Review the site's pricing information and request a scope for the actual advisory work.

A hypothetical consultation

Imagine a small home-services business running a search campaign in its actual service area. The owner sees a conversion count but the staff report that some inquiries are outside the services offered. The consultation brief includes the landing page, campaign goal and a summary of how staff handle calls and forms.

The consultant reviews an agreed sample and asks whether the conversion count includes telephone clicks and form attempts. The business has not yet tested the form's success signal. The preliminary recommendation is to verify the inquiry path and clarify service restrictions on the page before making major bidding changes. A deeper account and tracking review is offered as a separate option.

The owner receives a short action list and assigns a staff member to test the form with the website provider. No claim is made that the changes will guarantee a particular number of customers. This fictional scenario illustrates a decision process, not a real client result.

Decide what happens after the advice

Review the recommendations with the people who control the website, advertising account and budget. Confirm that the business can deliver any offer described in the campaign. If implementation is needed, agree on the changes, access, testing and rollback or pause process before work begins.

Document what changes and when. Later reports can be affected by seasonality, offer changes, budget adjustments or tracking updates. A better or worse metric after a consultation does not by itself establish that the advice caused the outcome. Use the evidence to make the next decision rather than manufacturing a success claim.

If the business chooses ongoing management, define the retainer's responsibilities separately: campaign changes, reporting, creative, landing-page coordination and lead feedback. If it chooses not to proceed, it should still retain its account access and the agreed consultation deliverables.

Prepare for a first campaign without an existing account

If the business has never advertised, the consultation can focus on readiness rather than account performance. Provide a description of the service, the geographic area you can serve, the website or landing page and the way an inquiry will be handled. Identify any time-sensitive constraints, such as a seasonal offer or a limited number of available appointments.

Ask the consultant what information would be needed to design a first test and what would remain uncertain. A useful answer might identify a need to verify the form, clarify pricing or research relevant search terms before choosing a campaign structure. It should not present an estimated number of clicks or sales as a guaranteed result. The business should also understand who will create and own the account if it chooses to proceed.

A first campaign may require several people: the business owner approves the offer and budget, a website developer handles the destination or form, and an advertising specialist configures the campaign. Agree on those responsibilities before setting a launch date. A delay in a website integration or approval can affect the schedule even if the campaign plan is otherwise ready.

Prepare for a discussion about an existing campaign

For an existing account, note the main changes made during the period you want to discuss. Include changes in service availability, advertising budget, landing-page URLs and the person managing the account. If the business has a change log, share an appropriate redacted version. This context helps the consultant avoid treating a difference between two periods as proof of a single cause.

Ask which data and settings the consultant can actually review under the proposed access. A read-only review may be appropriate for an initial assessment, but a specialist cannot verify an integration or modify a campaign without the necessary permissions and approval. If the consultation identifies a possible problem, request a description of the additional evidence needed before a change is made.

Check the follow-up process alongside the campaign

A business can receive relevant inquiries and still struggle to turn them into conversations if notifications go to an unmonitored inbox or staff do not know who owns the response. Bring a simple description of the current process: where calls and forms arrive, who checks them, what happens when the business is closed and how an unsuitable request is handled.

The consultant can flag a gap, but the business must decide how to staff and maintain the process. If a form test fails, coordinate with the website or form-service owner before interpreting advertising metrics. If the team cannot serve a particular location or service request, update the approved campaign and page information rather than treating every inquiry as equally useful.

Questions to ask before booking

Ask how much of the account or proposed campaign will be reviewed, what information is needed and whether the consultant requires access. Ask how they define a useful conversion, how they account for landing-page and follow-up issues, and what they will do if the data is insufficient.

Clarify the fee, pre-work, meeting length, written notes and follow-up boundary. If the business has a particular launch deadline or cannot share account access, say so early. A focused, transparent consultation can help the owner choose a practical next step even when the answer is to gather more evidence before spending.

Frequently asked questions

Is a consultation the same as ongoing Google Ads management?

No. A consultation provides agreed advice; ongoing management involves recurring work and should have a separate scope.

Can a consultant guarantee a return on ad spend?

No. Results depend on the offer, market, budget, landing page, measurement and follow-up. Avoid fixed guarantees.

Do I need to share my password?

No. Use individual platform permissions if access is required, and keep ownership and billing under business control.

What if my conversion tracking is unclear?

Document the uncertainty and agree on a controlled investigation. Do not treat every reported event as a qualified sale.

Should I run ads before my website is ready?

The destination and inquiry process should work and accurately describe the offer before paid traffic is sent. The exact readiness checks depend on the campaign.

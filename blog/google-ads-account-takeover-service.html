Taking Over an Existing Google Ads Account: A Buyer's Checklist

Learn how to scope a Google Ads account takeover, protect ownership, validate conversion tracking and agree on a safe transition.

Changing the person who manages an advertising account is not just a matter of giving someone a login. The business needs to retain control of its account, understand what is currently running and agree on how changes will be reviewed. A rushed transition can interrupt campaigns, obscure historical context or leave nobody certain whether a reported conversion represents a genuine inquiry.

A Google Ads account takeover service can provide a structured handover. Depending on the agreement, a freelancer may review access, campaigns, budgets, conversion tracking, landing pages and reporting before proposing changes. The work is distinct from building a new account and distinct from ongoing management. This guide helps a business specify the transition and compare proposals without assuming that an outside specialist can guarantee sales or prevent every disruption.

The existing Google Ads management guide explains wider campaign concepts. Here the focus is a specific buying situation: an account already exists and someone new needs to take responsibility for it.

Start by identifying the business owner of the account

The business should know which account is being managed, who has administrative access and who controls billing. Use the platform's access controls to invite the new provider with the appropriate role; do not send a shared password. Record the account identifier privately, verify the correct account with the relevant stakeholders and check that the business can access its own billing and campaign history.

If a previous agency used a manager account, understand the relationship between that manager account and the advertising account. A manager-account link is not a substitute for the business retaining appropriate access to its own assets. Consult the current Google Ads account access guidance and have the business's administrator make or approve access changes.

Avoid removing the outgoing provider before the business has secured access, collected the agreed handover and confirmed any contractual obligations. Equally, avoid leaving unnecessary access in place indefinitely. Establish a transition date and review permissions after the new manager has verified access. If ownership is disputed, resolve it through the appropriate platform and contractual processes; creating a new account is not automatically a safe solution.

Clarify why the management relationship is changing

A new provider needs context, not just a list of complaints. Has the business changed its offer? Is reporting unclear? Are campaigns running but inquiries do not match the desired customer? Has the previous manager left? The answers help define the first investigation and avoid treating every account as if it has the same problem.

Document what the business wants to preserve. A campaign may support a seasonal service, an important geographic area or a landing page that a sales team relies on. Ask the outgoing manager for known limitations, recent changes, planned launches and any tests already in progress. Historical notes can explain why an apparently unusual setting was chosen, even if the new specialist ultimately recommends changing it.

If there is a specific performance concern, describe it precisely and include the date range and data source. “We need more qualified inquiries” is a better starting point than assuming the account must be rebuilt. A drop in reported conversions could reflect real demand, tracking changes, budget changes, website issues or several factors at once. The first task is to gather evidence.

Define what the takeover includes

A takeover proposal should identify the accounts and campaigns covered, the markets and languages involved, the review period and the expected handover deliverables. Ask whether the specialist will provide a written audit, a transition plan, a meeting, implementation of approved changes and a follow-up check. Clarify whether advertising spend is separate from the management fee and who is authorized to adjust budgets.

Distinguish diagnosis from execution. A review may identify a conversion action that appears to count more than intended, but changing it can affect bidding and reporting. A proposal should explain who will investigate, who approves the change and how the team will validate it. If developer or analytics work is required, identify whether that work is included or requires a separate scope.

Agree on the initial operating mode. Some businesses may want the new manager to monitor existing campaigns while preparing recommendations. Others may need a planned pause because the current offer or landing page is unavailable. Neither approach is universally correct. The business should make the decision with the specialist after understanding possible effects on spend, data and customer acquisition.

Review access to connected assets

The advertising account may depend on assets outside the platform: the website, analytics configuration, tag manager, consent tools, call tracking, product feeds or a customer relationship management system. Inventory these connections and identify their owners. The new manager should request only the access necessary for the agreed work and explain why it is needed.

A read-only review may be sufficient for some initial checks. Implementing tags, changing landing pages or editing billing settings requires different permissions and may involve different people. Avoid giving broad administrative access simply because it is convenient. Keep a record of access granted, the purpose and the person responsible for removing it at the end of the engagement.

Review linked accounts and integrations for continuity. An old connection may still support a live conversion action or reporting workflow; removing it without understanding its role can create gaps. Conversely, an obsolete provider may retain access that is no longer appropriate. Verify the dependency before making changes and document the result.

Establish a baseline without treating it as a verdict

Before making substantial changes, capture the current state of campaigns, budgets, targeting, ads and conversion settings. Record the date and reporting filters. Export or document the information needed for a comparison, subject to the business's data handling requirements. A change log provides context for interpreting later results and helps the team recover if a setting was changed unintentionally.

Choose a relevant historical period, but do not assume that every comparison is equivalent. Seasonality, holidays, changes in product availability, tracking updates and website redesigns can all affect apparent performance. A new provider should ask about these events rather than declaring the previous work a failure based on a single dashboard view.

Review whether the data supports the business goal. An account may report a form submission while the sales team reports few suitable leads. That discrepancy could involve targeting, the form, follow-up, duplicate events or definitions. The specialist should state what is known, what needs validation and which people can help resolve the question.

Validate conversion tracking before optimizing to a number

Conversion tracking is central to decision-making, but a reported conversion is only as useful as its definition and implementation. List the actions currently counted, their purpose, whether they are primary or secondary in the account, and how they relate to the business's actual inquiry process. A click on a button, a successful form submission and a qualified sale are different events.

Test an inquiry path with permission. Does the form validate correctly? Does the business receive the message? Does the confirmation page or event occur only after success? Does a repeat click create duplicate reporting? Where calls or offline conversions are involved, determine what the current integration actually records. Do not place real customer data in a test unnecessarily.

If measurement depends on tags, consent settings or an analytics system, coordinate with the relevant site owner and qualified privacy advisers. Requirements vary by jurisdiction and implementation. The advertising specialist should not promise that every interaction can or should be tracked. Document limitations and avoid changing bidding inputs blindly while the measurement system is being investigated.

Review campaigns against the actual offer

Look at campaign objectives, geographic targeting, language settings, budgets, ad schedules and the relationship between ads and landing pages. A setting can be intentional even if it differs from a generic checklist. Ask whether each campaign corresponds to a real service, market and customer journey, and whether the business can fulfill the inquiries it receives.

Review search terms and targeting where the account provides the relevant data. Are ads reaching people looking for the intended service, or do available reports show recurring irrelevant themes? Potential negative keywords should be reviewed carefully: excluding a term without understanding its context can block useful demand. Document the reason for a change and test its effect over time.

A freelancer may recommend consolidating or separating campaigns, but a structural rebuild is not always the best first step. Existing campaigns contain history and may support current business operations. Ask what problem the proposed change solves, what data or settings will be affected, how the transition will be monitored and whether a less disruptive test is possible.

Examine ads and landing pages together

An ad can attract a relevant click but lead to a page that does not answer the visitor's question. Review the relationship between the message, the service offered and the destination. A landing page should describe the actual offer, make the next step clear and work on the devices your customers use. It should not promise a discount, service area or result that the business cannot honor.

Check forms, telephone links and confirmation messages. If the form asks for information that the sales team does not need, discuss whether the friction is justified. If the business needs additional detail to qualify a lead, make the reason clear and test the form on mobile. The existing landing-page guide discusses broader page principles.

Advertising copy should be reviewed for platform policy and factual accuracy. A freelancer can draft alternatives and organize testing, but the business owner should approve claims about its services, pricing and qualifications. A regulated business may need additional review by a qualified adviser before publishing.

Set a change-control plan

The handover should say which changes the new manager can make independently and which require approval. A routine correction to an obviously broken link may have a different approval path from a major budget increase, a new market or a change to primary conversion goals. Record agreed spending limits, communication channels and the person authorized to approve exceptions.

Use a change log that includes the date, affected campaign or setting, reason, owner and expected follow-up. For significant changes, note the previous state and how to reverse the change. Avoid changing many unrelated variables simultaneously when it would make the outcome difficult to interpret. Sometimes urgent corrections are necessary; the log should still explain why they were prioritized.

If the account uses automated bidding, understand which conversion signals and budgets are involved before altering them. Changes can affect how the account behaves, and a short observation window may not be enough to draw a conclusion. The specialist should describe the risks and monitoring plan rather than guaranteeing a rapid improvement.

Agree on reporting and communication

A practical transition report should show what was reviewed, what remains uncertain, which changes were approved, what was implemented and what needs the business's decision. It should separate platform metrics from business outcomes. A lead count, for example, does not show whether the sales team could contact the person or whether the inquiry matched the service.

Choose a reporting cadence that suits the account and the transition. An initial status update may be useful while access and tracking are being checked; later reporting can follow the ongoing management agreement. Include the date range, source, important changes and known limitations. Ask the specialist to explain unfavorable findings and unresolved problems as well as progress.

Define an escalation route for urgent issues such as unexpected spending or a broken landing page. The business must decide who can pause campaigns, who receives notifications and how to reach the relevant website or billing owner. Do not rely on a single person being available at all times unless that arrangement has been explicitly agreed.

Compare providers without relying on guarantees

Give prospective specialists the same brief: business goals, markets, current concerns, budget constraints and the access available for an initial review. Ask them to explain their takeover process and how they protect the account's history. Compare the deliverables, approval process, implementation responsibilities and exclusions. A proposal that includes only an audit is not directly comparable to one that includes a month of management and tracking validation.

Ask what happens if the provider finds an issue outside the agreed scope. A clear proposal can describe how additional work is quoted and approved. It should also explain who pays platform charges, whether third-party tools are required and what information the client receives when the relationship ends.

Be cautious of promises of a fixed return, guaranteed sales or immediate results without examining the account. The specialist can propose tests and explain the reasoning, but advertising results depend on the offer, market, competition, website, budget and many other factors. Honest uncertainty is more useful than a precise number without evidence.

A hypothetical transition

Imagine a small B2B service business whose previous contractor is leaving. The business owns its advertising account, but the owner is unsure which form submissions are being counted. Campaigns are still running and an important service page is scheduled for an update. The goal of the takeover is to preserve continuity while the business and the new specialist verify the measurement setup.

The owner grants appropriate access and asks the outgoing contractor for a change log and information about linked assets. The new specialist documents the current campaigns, checks the landing-page path and coordinates a test submission with the website owner. The team finds that a reported event needs further investigation; until its meaning is clear, they do not present the count as qualified sales. Any change to the conversion configuration is approved and recorded.

The specialist then presents prioritized recommendations, including any work that requires a developer. The business chooses which changes to approve and agrees on a reporting schedule. This example is fictional; it demonstrates a decision process rather than a result achieved for a real client.

Prepare for the first approval meeting

Before the first decision meeting, bring the list of current services, locations you can serve, available budget range and the person who can approve changes. Give the specialist a way to contact the website owner and the person who handles inquiries. If an offer is temporarily unavailable or has eligibility restrictions, highlight that before ad copy or targeting is changed. This prevents the team from optimizing toward inquiries the business cannot fulfill.

Ask the specialist to separate urgent risks from opportunities that can wait. For each proposed action, request the reason, the affected account components, the approval needed and the proposed validation. If a recommendation could interrupt spending or change the conversion signal used for bidding, ask what the business should expect during the transition and what monitoring is practical. Do not assume that pausing a campaign or changing a setting can be evaluated in isolation from the rest of the account.

Record decisions in a place both parties can access. If the owner rejects or postpones a recommendation, note the reason and any associated risk. This creates a useful shared history for the next review and avoids repeated discussions about a change that cannot yet be implemented.

Make the handover usable after the engagement

At the end of the takeover, the business should have an updated access inventory, a record of reviewed campaigns and connected assets, a change log, a list of unresolved issues and a plan for ongoing responsibilities. Confirm that the business retains the account and can access its history and billing. If a provider uses a proprietary reporting tool, request the underlying agreed deliverables in a format the business can use.

Review whether the proposed ongoing work matches your capacity. If the business cannot approve landing-page changes or respond to inquiries, campaign adjustments alone may not address the underlying problem. A useful plan identifies dependencies and assigns them to the appropriate people. It also distinguishes a one-time transition fee from ongoing management and advertising spend.

Before signing, ask for a written scope and compare it with the website's pricing information. Final fees should reflect the account's complexity, the markets involved and the actual work agreed. The best transition is not necessarily the one with the most changes; it is the one that protects ownership, validates important assumptions and makes future decisions easier.

Frequently asked questions

Should I create a new account when changing managers?

Not automatically. First confirm ownership, access, history and any contractual constraints. A new account may introduce reporting and continuity issues; assess the specific situation before deciding.

Should the previous manager be removed immediately?

Plan the transition first, secure business access and collect the agreed handover. Then remove unnecessary permissions at the agreed time and verify that connected assets still work.

Does the takeover include fixing conversion tracking?

Only if the agreement says so. Investigation, tag changes, website development and testing can require different access and responsibilities.

Can a new manager guarantee a better return?

No. They can review evidence and propose improvements, but outcomes depend on factors beyond the manager's control. Avoid specific guarantees without a sound basis.

Will campaigns need to stop during the handover?

Not necessarily. The right approach depends on current risks, business requirements and the agreed transition plan. The business should approve any pause or significant change.

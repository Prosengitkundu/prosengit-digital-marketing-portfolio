SEO Audit Services: What Should a Useful Report Include?

Learn how to commission an SEO audit, assess its evidence, compare recommendations, and turn findings into a practical implementation plan.

A website can look polished and still leave important questions unanswered. Can search engines reach the pages that matter? Does each service page explain a distinct offer? Can someone arriving from search understand what to do next? If your team cannot answer those questions, an SEO audit is a useful way to replace assumptions with evidence before committing to months of work.

The difficulty is that the word “audit” covers very different products. One provider may deliver an automated scan. Another may review a selection of pages, investigate search data, speak with your team and present a prioritized implementation plan. Both may call the result an SEO audit, but the decisions you can make from them will not be the same.

This guide explains what to ask for, how to interpret findings and how to make a report useful after the handover. It is written for people comparing SEO audit services, not for specialists seeking a list of every possible technical test. If you are still deciding which kind of help you need, start with the existing guide to choosing an SEO service.

Start with the decision, not the tool

A useful audit begins with a business question. A local clinic might need to understand whether potential patients can find its service pages and locations. A software company might want to know why relevant search visits rarely turn into qualified demonstrations. A new business may simply need confidence that its launch pages are accessible and describe the right services. These situations require different samples, evidence and follow-up work.

Write down the decisions you expect the audit to support. For example: should we repair the existing site, invest in new service pages, change a migration plan, or commission a deeper technical investigation? Also identify what the report will not decide. An SEO review cannot by itself establish the profitability of a product line or guarantee that a particular search position will be reached.

The value of a tool depends on the question. A crawler can reveal recurring response codes or missing page elements, but it cannot decide whether a service description reflects your current offer. Search Console can show aggregate search performance, but an apparent decline may be affected by seasonality, tracking changes or the way a report is filtered. Human review connects these observations with the business context and identifies where further investigation is necessary.

Define the boundaries before accepting a quote

A proposal should say which domain, subdomains, language versions and page types will be examined. If the website has hundreds of location pages, specify whether every URL will be reviewed or whether the specialist will inspect a representative sample. For an online store, clarify whether product, collection, search and filtered URLs are included. A small sample can be a sensible choice; a sample presented as a complete inspection is not.

Agree on the desired depth. A technical diagnostic, content review, local profile review and competitor study are related but distinct. You may not need all of them in the first engagement. For example, if a recent launch has urgent indexing problems, diagnosing the launch configuration can precede a broader content analysis. Conversely, a technically healthy site with unclear service pages may benefit more from audience and content analysis than another checklist of server settings.

Ask what access is necessary and why. A specialist may request read-only access to search and analytics accounts, a list of important URLs, and a contact who understands the site. Do not share passwords by email or grant administrative access simply because it is convenient. Google's advice on hiring an SEO recommends considering the scope of an audit and granting read access to Search Console at that stage rather than write access.

The agreement should also distinguish finding an issue from fixing it. A report might recommend redirect changes, but applying and testing those changes could require developer time, a separate quote and your approval. Establish the format of the handover, review meeting, revisions to the report and the person responsible for implementation. Ask whether the price includes a recheck after fixes or only the initial investigation.

What evidence should accompany each finding?

A finding is most useful when someone other than the auditor can reproduce or verify it. The report should name the affected URLs or page pattern, explain the observation, describe its possible consequence and show the evidence available. Where the issue is uncertain, it should say so. An example finding might identify several valuable service URLs returning an unexpected redirect and include the redirect destination and date checked. The next step would be to verify the intended destination with the site owner before changing anything.

For larger websites, a spreadsheet can hold URL-level details while the report explains the pattern. A business owner should not have to interpret hundreds of rows before understanding the decision. A practical summary might say that a navigation template links to old URLs, identify the template owner, and give a few examples. The full export remains available to the developer for implementation and testing.

Screenshots alone are not always sufficient. They can show what an auditor saw on a given day, but the underlying URL, device, measurement method and date matter. If a performance test is used, ask whether it represents a lab run, real-user data, or both. A single simulated result is useful for diagnosing a page under specified conditions; it is not a promise that all visitors experience the same speed.

Evidence also includes what could not be checked. An audit conducted without access to analytics should not make confident claims about conversion performance. A page unavailable behind a login cannot be assessed in the same way as a public page. A transparent limitations section protects your team from treating a partial view as a full diagnosis.

Crawlability and indexing: what to examine

Search engines need to discover and access pages before they can consider them for indexing. The auditor can inspect links, response codes, redirects, canonical signals, robots directives and the sitemap to understand whether important URLs are being presented consistently. The business decision is not “make every discovered URL indexable.” It is “make the intended public pages accessible and avoid sending conflicting signals.”

Consider a service business with a live service page that has accidentally inherited a noindex directive from staging. That directive deserves investigation because it may prevent the intended page from appearing in search. On the other hand, a private account page or an internal search result may intentionally be excluded. The report should explain the page's purpose before recommending a change.

Canonical URLs also require context. A canonical points to a preferred version, but it is not a command that guarantees search engines will select that URL. If a site contains near-identical pages for multiple campaign parameters, the recommendation may involve consistent internal links and canonical signals. If pages contain genuinely different services or serve different locations, consolidation should not be assumed merely because titles look similar.

The sitemap is a discovery aid, not a substitute for useful internal links or a guarantee of indexing. Ask the auditor to compare the sitemap with the intended public URLs and explain material discrepancies. A useful output identifies a sample, explains how the comparison was made and tells your team what to verify after changes.

On-page content and search intent

A good review asks whether a page answers the need behind a relevant search, not just whether a phrase appears in a title. For a service page, an auditor might examine whether the offer, intended customer, delivery scope, geographical availability and next action are understandable. If the page uses internal jargon while customers search for a familiar service name, the report can recommend clearer language without forcing awkward repetitions.

The existing on-page SEO checklist covers the mechanics. An audit should apply those principles to your specific pages: which title misrepresents the offer, which page lacks important service details, and which internal link would help a visitor reach the next relevant page? Recommendations should not prescribe the same title length or heading count for every URL without explaining the purpose.

The specialist should also identify overlapping pages carefully. Two pages mentioning “SEO” are not automatically duplicates. One may help a buyer choose a service while another specifies an audit deliverable. If the pages target the same audience and decision at the same stage, however, splitting information between them may make both less useful. Request a page-by-page recommendation: retain and differentiate, update, combine, or investigate further. A proposed consolidation should include the effect on internal links and redirects, not just a suggestion to delete one page.

Site experience and conversion paths

SEO audit services often include a usability review, but the scope varies. Agree whether the specialist will assess only a few mobile templates or conduct a broader accessibility and conversion review. Useful questions include whether content is readable at small widths, navigation works with a keyboard, images have appropriate alternatives, and forms provide understandable error messages. An SEO audit is not a substitute for a formal accessibility conformance assessment unless that is explicitly included.

Trace the path from a search landing page to an inquiry. A visitor who lands on an article may need a relevant service link before seeing a contact form. Someone who reaches a service page may want to understand the scope before requesting a quote. If the only next step is a generic button at the bottom of a long page, the audit might recommend a more relevant link near the explanation. Changes should support the reader rather than obscure the content with aggressive prompts.

Check whether your measurement can distinguish a meaningful inquiry from a button click. A form submission event might fire when a user clicks submit even if validation prevents sending the form. Conversely, an inquiry could arrive through email or telephone without appearing in a form report. The audit should describe these limitations and propose a validation plan rather than treating a single dashboard number as complete business revenue.

Performance: focus on representative user journeys

Page speed deserves attention, but a dashboard score is not the whole outcome. Select representative pages: for example, the homepage, a key service page, an article and the contact page. A slow template affecting every service page may deserve more attention than an isolated issue on an obsolete post. Capture the test conditions and note whether the evidence comes from real users, a simulated lab test or both.

Recommendations should identify the probable cause and the team capable of acting. Large unoptimized images, excessive third-party scripts or layout shifts caused by late-loading content may require different solutions. The developer should verify changes against the actual site rather than removing a script blindly: an analytics, booking or consent component may be important to business operations.

Ask for a before-and-after testing plan. Improvements may change with device, network, geography, browser caching and third-party behavior. Treat a proposed speed target as an agreed project goal, not a guaranteed ranking or sales outcome. The existing article on website speed and SEO provides additional background on why speed and usability need to be considered together.

Local and international considerations

If you serve a particular area, make sure the audit examines how the website describes that coverage. Your name, contact information and service details should be accurate on the site and on any profiles you control. A business with several genuine locations may need distinct information for each one; a service-area business should not invent an office address or pages for locations it does not serve. The existing local SEO guide explains the wider context.

International sites require different questions. Are language and regional versions genuinely useful for their intended audiences? Are currencies, contact expectations and service availability clear? If localized versions exist, an audit can examine whether the technical annotations and navigation are consistent with the actual content. A single English page serving several countries does not automatically require a separate page for every market. The decision should reflect a real audience need and the resources available to maintain the content.

When a specialist recommends market-specific URLs, ask what will be unique on each page and who will keep details current. A proposal that creates many nearly identical pages with different city or country names can increase maintenance without giving readers meaningful additional information. The international SEO guide is a useful starting point for broader market planning.

Priorities should reflect business impact and dependencies

A long list of findings is not a roadmap. Ask for priorities that consider the importance of affected pages, the likely consequence, confidence in the diagnosis, the effort involved and any dependencies. For example, fixing a blocked contact page may be more urgent than rewriting dozens of low-traffic descriptions. A sitewide template issue may deserve attention before individual page edits because one correction can affect many URLs.

Separate severity from effort. A high-impact issue may take significant planning, while a small correction may be easy but have limited effect. A useful report can group work into immediate risks, improvements that support important pages, and later opportunities. It should also identify any recommendation that depends on a developer, a content owner, an external vendor or a decision from management.

Avoid treating priority labels as guaranteed traffic forecasts. An auditor can explain why an issue matters and what evidence supports it, but search results respond to many factors beyond a single change. A clear statement such as “this may prevent the intended page from being indexed” is more useful than an unsupported claim that the fix will double leads.

What a handover should enable

At the end of an engagement, your team should be able to answer: what was reviewed, what was found, what should happen next, who owns each action and how will we verify it? A concise executive summary helps decision-makers. A detailed technical appendix helps implementers. A walkthrough lets both groups ask questions while the context is fresh.

For each recommendation, look for these practical details:

  • Affected scope: exact URLs, templates or account settings and the date checked.
  • Observation and evidence: what was seen and how it was established.
  • Business context: why the issue matters for the relevant audience or goal.
  • Recommended action: the proposed change, including dependencies or alternatives.
  • Owner and validation: who should act and what to test after implementation.

An audit is not automatically a content-writing assignment or a development contract. If you need rewritten service pages, a migration plan or implementation support, request those as explicit follow-on deliverables. For published starting points, compare the site's pricing information; the final scope and quote should be confirmed for your project.

Questions to ask before hiring

Ask the provider to explain the sample they would inspect and why it represents your site. Ask how they decide which recommendations matter, what data access they require and what they will do when evidence is incomplete. Request an example of the format of a finding or a redacted sample report, without assuming every previous engagement is comparable to yours.

Discuss communication. Will there be an initial briefing, a written handover and a review call? Who will answer questions from the developer? If your staff work across time zones, agree on a practical response and review process. The exact cadence should fit the project rather than being inferred from a marketing page.

Be cautious of guarantees of first-place rankings, secret relationships with search engines or a requirement to buy unrelated links. Google explicitly warns that no one can guarantee a number-one ranking. Ask for understandable explanations and retain ownership of your website and accounts. You should be able to stop an engagement without losing access to your data or core assets.

Prepare a useful audit brief

A short brief can save time and help a freelancer give a more accurate proposal. Include your website URL, the services or products that matter most, your target locations and the type of customer you want to reach. Identify recent site migrations, redesigns, major content changes or tracking changes. Mention any known constraints, such as a planned launch date, a limited development budget or a platform controlled by another vendor.

List the pages you consider important and explain why. If a page receives inquiries through several channels, do not assume search is the only reason it matters. Share questions you want answered, rather than prescribing a particular tool. If the previous provider delivered a report, include it with dates and explain which recommendations were implemented. This prevents repeating work while allowing the new specialist to verify whether old findings still apply.

A useful closing question for the brief is: “What will I be able to decide after receiving this report?” If the proposal cannot answer that, refine the scope before buying. The right audit should leave you with a defensible next step, whether that is a small fix, a deeper investigation or a longer-term SEO engagement.

From report to implementation

After the handover, review findings with the people who will make changes. Confirm whether the proposed fix is technically feasible, whether it affects other parts of the site, and whether the business agrees with any content changes. For significant changes, record the original state, prepare a rollback plan and test on staging where appropriate. Keep an action log with a responsible person and a verification date.

Not every recommendation should be implemented immediately. A potential improvement can be deferred if it has a low business impact, relies on unavailable resources or conflicts with an imminent launch. Record the reason so it can be revisited when circumstances change. Equally, reject advice that is unsupported or inconsistent with your business requirements; ask the specialist to clarify the evidence before proceeding.

Once changes are live, recheck the affected pages and monitor appropriate measures over a suitable period. Search systems may take time to process changes, and other marketing activities can influence results. Track what changed and when so that later observations can be interpreted cautiously. The purpose is to learn from the work, not to attribute every fluctuation to a single adjustment.

A practical example: a service business after a redesign

Imagine a fictional consultancy that has recently replaced its website. Its owner notices fewer inquiries and asks for an SEO audit. Before accepting the conclusion that search rankings caused the change, a specialist compares the timing of the redesign with search performance, form behavior and major page changes. The investigation is scoped to the main service pages, contact path and redirect map; it does not claim to audit every historical URL.

The review might find that a former service URL redirects to the homepage rather than the closest current service page, while the new contact form produces an unclear confirmation. These are different problems: one concerns the continuity of an old page, and the other concerns a visitor's ability to complete an inquiry. The report should provide the affected URLs, explain the limits of the available evidence and ask the site owner to confirm the intended destination and form behavior.

The team can then assign a developer to check the redirect, ask the contact-form provider to verify delivery, and test the path from the service page on both desktop and mobile. If analytics changed during the redesign, the team should reconcile tracking before interpreting the apparent decline. This scenario illustrates a decision process, not a real client result or a guarantee that the same fixes will help another site.

How to choose the right depth of audit

A focused audit is appropriate when you have a specific decision and a constrained set of pages. You might need a pre-launch check of a new landing page or an investigation into a known indexing issue. A broader audit is more appropriate when several departments need a shared view of technical, content and measurement priorities. Ask each provider to describe what is excluded so you can compare proposals fairly.

If a broad proposal exceeds your budget, consider a phased scope instead of accepting an undocumented shallow scan. Phase one can identify urgent risks and the evidence needed for a deeper review. A second phase can examine selected page types or implement approved fixes. The sequence should be documented, with costs and responsibilities agreed before work begins.

A report is only the beginning of improvement. Choose an engagement that gives your team enough clarity to take the next action, and make sure the next action is realistic for your resources. A concise, well-supported report can be more useful than a lengthy export that nobody is responsible for implementing.

Frequently asked questions

Is an SEO audit the same as ongoing SEO management?

No. An audit is a defined investigation and handover. Ongoing management may include recurring analysis, implementation, content planning and reporting. Agree on either scope separately.

Can an audit guarantee higher rankings?

No. It can identify issues and opportunities, but rankings and business results depend on many factors. Avoid providers who promise a specific search position.

Do I need to give an auditor full administrator access?

Not necessarily. Agree on the minimum access needed. Read-only access is often appropriate for an initial review; request a separate explanation and approval for any access needed to implement changes.

How long does an audit take?

The timeline depends on site size, access, the depth of review and stakeholder availability. Ask for a written schedule and the assumptions behind it rather than relying on a universal deadline.

Will the audit include fixes?

Only if the proposal says so. Clarify which changes are included, who approves them, how they are tested and whether follow-up verification is part of the price.
